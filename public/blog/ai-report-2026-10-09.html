Executive Thesis

The debate over AI valuations is ultimately a debate over who will retain the economic value created by the technology. Rapid adoption can generate enormous revenue while leaving model providers with modest returns if competition compresses prices and infrastructure absorbs the cash.

OpenAI's proposed $1.4 trillion valuation therefore deserves a stricter test than subscriber growth alone. The key variables are revenue quality, profit after all operating costs, infrastructure obligations, customer retention and the availability of alternative models.

The telecom analogy is useful under a specific condition: AI access becomes a widely available service whose suppliers compete on price, reliability and capacity. It becomes less useful where a provider owns deeply integrated workflows, differentiated capabilities and durable customer relationships.

2. The Earnings Required to Support $1.4 Trillion

For an illustrative steady-state valuation, assume investors eventually accept a price-to-earnings multiple of 30×. At a $1.4 trillion equity value, the company would need approximately $46.7 billion of annual net profit. The resulting revenue requirement depends heavily on the achievable net margin.

Assumed net marginRevenue required for $46.7B net profit

20%$233.3B
30%$155.6B
40%$116.7B

Required net profit = $1,400B ÷ 30 = $46.7B
Required revenue at a 20% net margin = $46.7B ÷ 20% = $233.3B

These are assumptions, not OpenAI guidance. The $233 billion figure follows from a 20% net margin and a 30× earnings multiple. It is not an unavoidable revenue threshold, and it does not include a discounted forecast of the years required to reach that scale.

For comparison, at $50 billion of revenue and a hypothetical 20% margin, profit would be $10 billion. A $1.4 trillion valuation would then equal 140× that profit. This calculation highlights the growth embedded in the price; it does not claim that OpenAI currently earns a 20% net margin.

This exercise is a test of scale. It should not be presented as proof that OpenAI must acquire a billion individual subscribers.

Competition can affect more than customer count. It can reduce realized prices, increase customer-acquisition spending and force continued investment in model quality. Conversely, strong integration with company processes can make switching more costly and support better retention.

7. Why the Telecom Comparison Matters

Telecom provides a useful economic reference when customers buy recurring access to capacity, several suppliers offer broadly comparable service and maintaining quality requires ongoing investment. AI can share these characteristics, including a material cost associated with fulfilling additional usage.

Economic factorPotential implication for an AI provider
Substitutable serviceGreater pricing pressure and weaker retention
Recurring infrastructure requirementsLess cash left after investment or cloud payments
High fixed costsUtilization becomes central to returns
Deep workflow integrationPotentially higher switching costs and a valuation premium
Strong growthMay justify a premium while it persists

A subscription is a billing method, not a valuation category. A provider with rapid growth, high incremental margins and durable customer relationships can command different multiples from a mature network operator. The analogy becomes stronger if competition erodes differentiation while infrastructure costs remain substantial.

Moving infrastructure spending to a cloud partner does not eliminate its economic cost. It may convert owned assets and capital expenditure into service payments or contractual commitments.

8. Cash Flow Is the More Demanding Test

Deutsche Telekom illustrates why cash generation and financing matter. For the first half of 2026, it reported €59.8 billion in revenue, €10.7 billion in free cash flow after leases and €138.4 billion in net debt. Its free-cash-flow measure excludes spectrum investment, among other defined items, so it is not directly comparable with a model provider's net income. [7]

A sound comparison distinguishes equity value from enterprise value, which includes net debt and other relevant claims. High debt can make an operator's equity-value-to-revenue ratio look low without producing an equally low enterprise-value ratio.

For AI, the central test is cash left after serving customers, developing models, maintaining required compute capacity and meeting financing obligations. Adjusted profit that omits recurring economic costs would provide a weaker basis for valuation.

In a scenario where model-provider financing becomes more constrained, orders or deployment schedules could be affected elsewhere in the chain. This is a transmission mechanism to monitor, not evidence that every supplier is experiencing deteriorating demand.

11. Firmus Shows That Financing Is a Separate Constraint

Firmus withdrew its planned Australian IPO on October 9 after seeking a valuation of approximately A$44 billion, or about US$31 billion. The cancellation illustrates that ambitious AI infrastructure plans still depend on investor acceptance of valuation and execution risk. It does not by itself establish that AI demand has collapsed. [8]

The distinction matters: a project may serve a growing market yet struggle to raise capital at its preferred price. Financing constraints can affect deployment even when the underlying technology remains useful.

13. Foxorox Conclusion

The telecom analogy identifies a credible valuation risk. If AI models become readily interchangeable while delivering them remains capital-intensive, competition may push economics toward a recurring-access business with constrained margins. In that scenario, a 28× revenue-run-rate valuation would require especially strong justification.

The billion-subscriber calculation is useful as a stress test but cannot carry the whole argument. OpenAI already disclosed material enterprise revenue, and higher-value business services can change the customer arithmetic. The more decisive issue is whether those revenues generate durable profit and cash after infrastructure costs.

The key question for investors is how much value the provider retains. AI can become economically important without every AI-related company earning returns sufficient to justify its valuation.
