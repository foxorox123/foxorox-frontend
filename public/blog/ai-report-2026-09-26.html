Is AI Starting to Threaten Roper Technologies' Business Model?

We think there is a much bigger question surrounding Roper Technologies than its latest earnings or current share price: What happens to its business when AI agents start doing the work that customers currently pay Roper's software to perform? Multitask delivered by AI agents is much more efficent than Roper human service and Roper software.

Roper owns a portfolio of specialized software businesses, including Deltek, Aderant, Vertafore, DAT and CentralReach. These companies operate in industries where customers depend on their software for everyday operations. Historically, that has been an attractive business model. Customers pay recurring subscription fees, switching providers is difficult, and Roper benefits from predictable revenue.

But AI agents could change those economics. Imagine a company that currently pays for 100 software licenses. If AI agents can perform much of the work previously handled by employees, will that company still need 100 licenses? Or will it eventually need just 20 licenses and a handful of AI agents? It is obvious that cost of people is higher then AI agents. There is another possibility. Customers might continue using Roper's software to store and manage their data while relying on external AI agents to perform the actual work. In that scenario, Roper could retain its customers but lose some of its pricing power and opportunities to sell additional software. This raises three important questions: 1. Will customers start reducing their traditional software subscriptions while continuing to use Roper's platforms primarily as databases? 2. Will the additional revenue generated by Roper's own AI agents be enough to compensate for potential losses in traditional software subscriptions? 3. What happens if independent AI providers gain access to the data and workflows that currently give Roper its competitive advantage? These questions are particularly relevant to businesses such as Deltek, Aderant and Vertafore, where AI agents could automate administrative work, reporting, document processing and other tasks that currently require human interaction with specialized software. However, there is an important distinction between a potential threat and something that is already happening. During a September 2026 Goldman Sachs conference, Roper's CEO, Neil Hunn, addressed concerns about AI reducing the number of paid software users. According to management, the company has not yet observed a significant decline in customer subscriptions attributable to AI, and enterprise customer retention remains around 95%. Roper is also developing its own AI products. The company intends to introduce additional subscription packages and usage-based pricing, allowing customers to pay for work performed by AI agents. In other words, Roper is trying to benefit from the same technological shift that could threaten its existing business. The problem is that this transition is still in its early stages. Roper's second-quarter 2026 results showed approximately 9% revenue growth, but organic growth was only around 5%. Adjusted earnings and free cash flow continued to increase. These figures do not demonstrate that AI is already causing customers to abandon Roper's software. Nevertheless, investors may be concerned about what happens over the next several years rather than what happened in the latest quarter. My main concern is that Roper could continue reporting respectable financial results while the long-term economics of some of its software businesses gradually deteriorate. A customer does not necessarily have to abandon Roper's software for AI to become a problem. Simply reducing the number of paid users, negotiating lower subscription fees or moving additional functionality to external AI providers could affect future revenue growth. That is why I believe the market's concerns deserve attention, even though there is currently insufficient evidence to conclude that AI is responsible for Roper's declining share price. The next few quarters should provide more information. I would pay particular attention to organic subscription growth, customer retention, revenue generated by new AI products and any changes in how Roper charges customers for its software. The central question is no longer whether Roper can develop AI agents. It is whether Roper can capture enough of the economic value created by those agents to protect its existing business model.

Executive Thesis

Roper is not a conventional industrial conglomerate anymore. It is primarily a portfolio of niche, mission-critical software and technology businesses acquired and managed for long-term free cash flow per share.

Yet its shares have suffered a major valuation reset. The important distinction: operating performance is not collapsing, but the market has become less willing to capitalize software cash flows at historical premium multiples.

The central question: Is the falling stock signaling deterioration in Roper's economic moat, or is the market repricing a still-profitable acquisition-driven software portfolio because of AI, organic-growth expectations and capital allocation risk?

5. Network Software: Acquisitions Mask Slower Underlying Growth

Network Software reported 11.8% revenue growth but only 3.6% organic growth. The difference was driven largely by acquired businesses, including Subsplash. Operating margin fell by 290 basis points, from 43.9% to 41.0%.

The company identified acquisition-related expense profiles and revenue mix, including payments and freight-related platform activities. These may be manageable, but they challenge the idea that every additional dollar of acquired revenue immediately preserves historical margins.

6. Technology Enabled Products: Cost Inflation and Mix

Technology Enabled Products grew organically by 7.1%, helped by medical products. Yet its gross margin fell from 58.6% to 56.9%, while operating margin fell from 35.4% to 33.3%.

Roper attributed pressure to higher input costs at Neptune, product mix at NDI and Verathon, and weaker operating leverage in water-meter technology. This is a real operating issue, not merely market speculation.

7. AI: Opportunity and Threat at the Same Time

Roper argues that its proprietary data, domain expertise, installed customer relationships and mission-critical workflows create a foundation for differentiated AI products. Management reported multiple new AI product launches in Q2 and discussed commercialization through new subscription offerings.

However, investors are also reassessing the durability of traditional software economics. AI can accelerate software development, reduce implementation barriers, change user interfaces and put pressure on vendors that sell narrowly defined workflow functionality.

This is an industry risk scenario, not evidence that Roper has already lost customers to AI competitors.

The key test: Can Roper turn AI features into higher recurring revenue and customer retention faster than AI changes the competitive landscape and its own development costs?

8. Deltek, DAT and End-Market Cyclicality

Some portfolio businesses are exposed to customers' spending cycles. Government contracting software can be sensitive to procurement delays and uncertainty; freight matching is influenced by trucking volumes and spot-market activity.

Management has discussed earlier headwinds in Deltek's government-contracting market and DAT's freight market, although freight indicators showed signs of improvement in 2026.

The portfolio is diversified, but it is not immune to the real economy or government spending timing.

9. Acquisitions: Roper's Growth Engine Has a Cost

Roper's historical strategy is to acquire specialized businesses with durable market positions and attractive cash generation. Acquisitions such as CentralReach, Orchard Software and Subsplash contributed to recent reported growth.

The economic challenge is to generate returns above the cost of acquisition capital without compromising organic growth or margins.

With acquisition-driven businesses, investors should track free cash flow per share, return on invested capital, net debt and organic growth together. EPS growth alone is insufficient.

13. Valuation: The Critical Accounting Trap

Roper's reported GAAP earnings include gains and other items that can materially distort trailing P/E. Therefore a screen displaying a trailing P/E around 15x should not be interpreted as 15x recurring earnings.

The more useful starting point is management's full-year adjusted EPS guidance, while remembering that adjusted EPS also excludes costs such as acquired-intangible amortization.

Do not mix GAAP P/E, adjusted P/E and enterprise-value ratios as though they measure the same earnings base.

22. Foxorox Conclusion

Roper's falling share price should not be mistaken for collapsing fundamentals. The company increased 2026 guidance, generated $447 million of adjusted quarterly free cash flow and continued substantial repurchases. At the same time, organic growth is materially below reported growth, two segment margins declined, and the acquisition-led model must prove its resilience in a changing AI software market.

Foxorox currently identifies a bearish trend. Fundamental analysis explains why the stock can remain under pressure even when quarterly earnings are positive: the market is reassessing the premium previously paid for predictable vertical-software compounding.

The decisive variables are future organic growth, margin recovery, AI monetization and cash-flow returns on capital deployment.
